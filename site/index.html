Design the chip your model needs.

ChipLeap explores the architecture before anyone writes RTL: memory system, external memory, dataflow, compute and process node, each choice explained. Then it generates the hardware, a RISC-V SoC on X-HEEP or AXI4 IP for any host, and checks it down to a routed layout. Or bring RTL of your own: the same flow takes it to a whole chip.

Watch it design a chip

Llama 3.2 1B decoding at 5 tokens per second, within 8 mm² and 400 mW. The agent starts from a bare microcontroller, names what limits it, tries every single change, keeps the best and says why. The floorplan is drawn to scale at every step. This run evaluated 2,154 designs in under 5 seconds.

  1. Start

    Start from a bare microcontroller

    A CV32E20 at 100 MHz with two 32 KiB banks on IHP 130 nm. The CPU does every multiply itself, and the 642 MiB of weights and KV cache do not fit in a 64 MiB HyperRAM.

    0.0036 tokens per second

  2. Decision 1

    Move to 65 nm, 200 MHz and LPDDR2

    A newer node allows faster clocks and memory interfaces at lower energy. Before this step 100% of the time went to compute.

    0.0036 to 0.019 tokens per second, 1.11e+03 to 410 mJ per token, 4.97 to 3.13 mm², now buildable

  3. Decision 2

    Add a GEMV engine

    Matrix multiplies dominate and the CPU does them one MAC at a time. Before this step 100% of the time went to compute.

    0.019 to 1.70 tokens per second, 410 to 86.5 mJ per token, 3.13 to 3.69 mm²

  4. Decision 3

    Move to 28 nm, 400 MHz and LPDDR4X

    A newer node allows faster clocks and memory interfaces at lower energy. Before this step 75% of the time went to the accelerator's ports.

    1.70 to 3.30 tokens per second, 86.5 to 29.1 mJ per token, 3.69 to 2.50 mm²

  5. Decision 4

    Move to 16 nm, 800 MHz

    A newer node allows faster clocks and memory interfaces at lower energy. Before this step 76% of the time went to the accelerator's ports.

    3.30 to 6.18 tokens per second, 29.1 to 28 mJ per token, 2.50 to 1.37 mm², meets every target

  6. Decision 5

    Switch to LPDDR5

    A slower interface can be cheaper in area and energy per bit. Before this step 78% of the time went to the accelerator's ports.

    throughput unchanged at 6.18 tokens per second, 28 to 22.5 mJ per token, 1.37 to 1.03 mm²

  7. Decision 6

    Make the banks 16 KiB

    Unused SRAM costs area and leakage. Before this step 78% of the time went to the accelerator's ports.

    throughput unchanged at 6.17 tokens per second, 1.03 to 1.00 mm²

    No single change lowers the energy further, so the agent stops with a 4 x 8 GEMV engine next to a CV32E20 at 800 MHz in 16 nm, fed by LPDDR5: 6.17 tokens per second at 22.6 mJ per token in 1.00 mm². Its weights now arrive as fast as the accelerator's ports take them in, so more ports would raise the token rate but not lower the energy per token.

One loop, from requirement to RTL

Each stage writes a file the next one reads, so every result can be opened, edited and checked.

  1. DescribeA YAML spec: the model, context and precision; tokens per second, area and power; the nodes and memories you can use.
  2. Sizechipleap require turns the target into bandwidth, MAC rate and capacity, and rules out what cannot deliver.
  3. ExploreThe explainable agent plus a seeded global search: a decision log, a Pareto front, a recommended design.
  4. GenerateAn X-HEEP configuration, the accelerator in SystemVerilog, a register map, a C driver, a testbench, a datasheet.
  5. VerifySimulation against a golden model, cycles against the model, synthesis on IHP SG13G2, X-HEEP's own checks.

Every check is IHP's own: the DRC deck on the whole die with its density rules, the LVS deck down to the macros' transistors, the antenna deck. How a chip is built and checked.

Analog blocks come from aispice

ChipLeap covers the digital side. Its analog counterpart, aispice, sizes analog blocks on the same PDK, draws their layouts with generators checked by IHP's DRC and LVS decks, and measures their specs before and after extraction. The two hand blocks to each other as macro packages, and both check a package with the same rules before placing it.

aispice The package format
  1. aispiceSizes the block at every corner, draws it, checks DRC and LVS, extracts it.
  2. strongarm.macroGDS, LEF, liberty, Verilog, SPICE, measured specs and the DRC and LVS evidence, named by one macro.toml.
  3. ChipLeapChecks the package, places it in the core and runs LVS down to its transistors.

The arithmetic that sets the limit

At batch one, decode reads every weight once per token, so the external memory sets a ceiling no amount of compute can raise. Pick a model and a memory.

Where it fits

Agentic EDA suites from Cadence, Synopsys and Siemens, and startups such as ChipAgents, Chipmind and SigmanticAI, mostly start from a specification or RTL on licensed tools. Workload-to-chip companies such as Architect Labs and Cognichip are closed services. Academic tools such as Timeloop, ZigZag and LLMCompass model accelerators without generating hardware. ChipLeap works on the step before the specification, and hands one over.

Python 3.10 or newer. Verilator, Yosys, LibreLane, the IHP PDK and the other tools are optional: chipleap verify uses what it finds and skips the rest with the reason, and chipleap doctor lists it.

Questions

Does it need a commercial EDA licence?

No. Simulation and lint use Verilator, synthesis uses Yosys with sv2v, place and route uses LibreLane, the technology data comes from the IHP SG13G2 open PDK, and X-HEEP's own generator checks the configuration. The model, the explorer and the generators need none of them. If you do have Cadence, Synopsys or Siemens tools, the same checks run on them, on your machine or on a design server, and chipleap tech add reads your own PDK.

Can I use it on RTL I wrote?

Yes. chipleap project init writes a project file for a directory of Verilog or SystemVerilog with cocotb tests, and chipleap verify runs the same checks the generated hardware gets, then the steps after the layout and, with a chip section, the whole chip. docs/projects.md describes the project file and each step.

How accurate is the model?

For the accelerator, the model predicts the generated RTL's cycle count exactly in simulation without memory stalls, and within a few percent with random stalls. Its logic area is within 3% on average of Yosys synthesis on IHP SG13G2, and the synthesized logic runs faster than the clock the model allows there. Memories, CPU and energy rest on technology data that is measured for IHP SG13G2 and scaled from published figures for the other nodes, and reports say which is which. docs/model.md lists every number and its source.

Do I have to use RISC-V or X-HEEP?

No. X-HEEP is the default because it is an open, configurable RISC-V microcontroller from EPFL with a Python configuration API and several chips taped out, and its generator validates what ChipLeap writes. With platform: standalone ChipLeap generates the accelerator alone as AXI4 IP, with a plain C driver, for an Arm, RISC-V or FPGA SoC; its AXI interfaces are simulated bit-exact like the rest.

Can I use my own model or network?

Yes. Any decoder-only transformer can be described by its shape, and other networks as convolutions, depthwise convolutions, matrix multiplies and element-wise operators.

What does the generated accelerator not do yet?

It computes int8 activations against int8, int4 or int2 weights with int32 results. Requantization, activation functions and int4 group scales run on the CPU, and the model counts them there. It has been placed and routed on IHP SG13G2 with clean DRC and LVS, but not taped out.

Has a ChipLeap chip been taped out?

Not yet. The chip on this page passes IHP's DRC deck on the whole die with its density rules, its LVS deck down to the transistors of the macros, antenna checks, timing at every corner through the pads, and the public parts of a shuttle's precheck. Fabrication is the step still missing.

Is it affiliated with EPFL, IHP or any company named here?

No. ChipLeap is an independent open-source project.
